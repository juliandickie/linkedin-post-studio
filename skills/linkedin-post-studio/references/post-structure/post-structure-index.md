# Post structure index

Every high-performing LinkedIn post follows a predictable architecture. The three-part skeleton below is the foundation. The framework selection map and sub-files that follow it help you choose the right copywriting structure for each goal.

## Three-part skeleton

**Hook (lines 1–3).** The hook stops the scroll and earns the "See more" click. Nothing else matters if the hook fails. Justin Welsh frames the full post as "trailer, content, recap" — the hook is the trailer. It announces what the post is about and creates enough curiosity that the reader expands. A hook that overpromises and underdelivers is worse than a weak hook: the semantic gap triggers a quality penalty at Stage 1.

**Body.** The body delivers on the hook's promise through one of three structural approaches: teach (give a framework or lesson), story (narrate a sequence with a lesson embedded), or argue (defend a contrarian position with evidence). Most top-performing posts blend two of these. The body is where the hook's implicit contract is honored.

**CTA.** The CTA tells the reader exactly what to do next. It must be singular — one action per post. Competing CTAs yield zero action. The CTA should align with the post's primary goal: comment prompts drive reach, save prompts drive authority signals, DM keyword triggers drive warm leads.

## Above-the-fold rules

The text visible before the "See more" click is the highest-value real estate in any post. LinkedIn shows roughly 140 characters on mobile (2 lines) and 210 characters on desktop (3 lines).

**Character budgets per line:**

- Line 1: maximum 62 characters
- Line 2: maximum 62 characters
- Line 3 (the "hook line"): maximum 50 characters

The third line is the last thing visible before "See more." It must create a curiosity gap strong enough to earn the expansion click. Chris Donnelly's published guide formalizes these limits.

**Structural patterns.** Two layouts dominate high-performing hooks:

- `[LINE / GAP / LINE]` — opening statement, single-word pivot, hook line
- `[LINE / LINE / GAP]` — two-line setup, then the pivot as the hook line

The GAP is a one-to-three word pivot: "Here's why", "Then...", "Until now." It acts as a fulcrum that makes the third line feel like the beginning of an answer the reader hasn't received yet.

**Formatting in the hook.** Avoid bold, italics, and emoji in the first three lines. Clean typography performs better and reads less performative. Decorated hooks signal effort rather than confidence.

**Jasmin Alic's re-hook technique.** Place a second hook line immediately after the fold — the first line the reader sees after clicking "See more." This line "slams the door behind the reader," committing them before they've processed the full body. The re-hook is typically a sharp one-liner that sharpens the tension or reframes the opening claim.

## Body structure rules

**Line density.** One idea per line when possible. Blank line between every paragraph. This is not stylistic preference — it is a mobile readability requirement. Jasmin Alic's "visual suffocation" test: if the post doesn't breathe on a phone screen, it is too dense and readers abandon before the CTA.

**Broetry.** Every-line-a-fragment formatting works for story beats and punch-lists. It fails for conceptual content that needs connective tissue. Post-2024 audiences recognize broetry that hides a lack of substance and penalize it.

**Paragraph length.** 1–3 sentences maximum. The AuthoredUp benchmark for high-engagement formatting is 14+ short paragraphs in a 1,500-character post.

**Sentence length.** 10–19 words average. Posts above grade 6 reading level (Hemingway Editor) see 35%+ less reach because they fail mobile scannability.

**Bullet patterns.** Four types in common use:

- Arrows (→, ↳) for process sequences and sub-points
- Checkmark/X binary lists (✅ ❌) for comparison and decision framing
- Numbered sequences for ranked or ordered items
- Plain dashes for neutral lists without implied hierarchy

## CTA patterns

**Comment-bait (thoughtful, not banned).** Prompts that invite a real answer rather than a one-word reaction:

- "What would you add to this list?"
- "Which of these hit hardest?"
- "What's your experience?"

Avoid "Like if you agree" — LinkedIn's classifier flags it as engagement bait and demotes reach.

**Save-bait.** Saves carry 5–10x the algorithmic weight of a like in 2025–2026:

- "Save this for later."
- "Bookmark for your next launch."
- "Screenshot and pin to your desk."

**Tag-bait.** Use sparingly. Tag prompts that feel forced are demoted. "Tag the founder in your life who needs to read this" works; "Tag 3 people" is a flagged phrase.

**DM keyword triggers.** Combine public comment volume with warm DM list growth:

- "Comment HOOKS and I'll DM the full cheat sheet."
- "Type PLAYBOOK and I'll send the template."

**Lead magnet drops.** Link to PDF, Notion template, or Loom video in the first comment — not in the post body. A link in the post body with a preview card carries the worst reach penalty of any placement.

**Soft vs. hard CTA.** Soft closes (pure observation, no ask) work every 8–10 posts to provide contrast and avoid audience fatigue. Hard CTAs (book a call, join the cohort) should appear weekly or less.

**The one-CTA rule.** One CTA per post. Every additional ask dilutes attention and reduces the probability that any action is taken.

## Framework selection map (goal → framework)

| Goal | Primary frameworks | Secondary frameworks |
|---|---|---|
| lead-gen | PAS, AIDA, PASO | FAB, 4Ps |
| thought-leadership | SLAY, framework-post (custom) | 4Ps, STAR |
| brand-awareness | BAB, STAR, SLAY, Hook-Story-Offer | — |
| community | Hook-Story-Offer | Open question / conversation-seeding |

When the goal is unclear, default to SLAY — it works across the widest range of content types.
