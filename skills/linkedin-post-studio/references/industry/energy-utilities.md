# Energy and Utilities — Content patterns and compliance

## What works

Energy and utility companies systematically underperform on LinkedIn through corporate channels. The gap between company page reach and personal profile reach in this sector is the widest of any industry covered here — consistently 5x or greater in favor of individual employees. The structural opportunity is shifting content ownership from the communications team to engineers, project managers, and operations leads who publish from personal accounts.

**Employee-led technical explainers.** Engineers and project managers posting from personal profiles on grid modernization, renewable integration, battery storage, or transmission infrastructure consistently outperform the same company's official posts on identical topics. The credibility comes from the specific role: a transmission engineer explaining line loss optimization at the per-mile level has authority that no corporate communications team can replicate. These posts attract a self-selecting audience of other practitioners, policy professionals, and investors who are tracking the sector closely.

**Vertical video for infrastructure projects.** Short clips — 30 to 90 seconds — demystifying complex energy systems perform well because public understanding of grid infrastructure is low while public interest is high. How a transmission substation integrates intermittent wind and solar, how a grid-scale battery responds to frequency events at 100-millisecond intervals, how demand response programs shed load during peak events — these are visual stories with no good public documentation outside academic literature. The creator who explains them concretely captures a specific audience that is underserved.

**Policy and regulatory commentary tied to named developments.** FERC Order 2023, IRA production tax credit provisions, state PUC decisions on grid interconnection queues, NERC reliability assessments — this content attracts policy professionals, utility executives, and clean energy investors who treat LinkedIn as a real-time regulatory tracking layer. The post must cite the specific order or rule, explain the material implication, and distinguish between what changed and what did not.

**Sustainability case studies with numbers.** Emissions reduction, MW capacity added, cost-per-kWh delivered, LCOE comparisons — content that leads with specific outcomes and explains the technical path to achieving them generates engagement from practitioners and investors alike. "We brought 120 MW of utility-scale solar online in 14 months at $38/MWh LCOE" is a post. "We are committed to a clean energy future" is not.

## What fails

**Greenwashing-adjacent language.** "Committed to a sustainable future" without a specific emissions number, project milestone, or regulatory milestone attached generates skepticism from the sector's LinkedIn audience, which includes analysts and journalists who track corporate sustainability claims. Vague commitments perform worse than no sustainability content at all because they invite critical commentary.

**Corporate milestone posts without technical context.** "We are proud to announce the completion of [project]" without explaining what the project does, how it changes the grid or the emissions picture, and what the specific numbers look like is content that the practitioner audience ignores. The announcement is the frame; the technical content is the post.

**Safety messaging that reads as HR-driven.** "Safety is our core value" posts with stock imagery or posed hard-hat photographs signal nothing about actual operational culture. The energy sector LinkedIn audience recognizes performative safety content and does not engage with it. Effective safety content is specific: a near-miss debrief with the procedural change that followed, or a post-incident technical review with extracted lessons.

## Industry-specific hook adaptations

Energy and utilities hooks perform best when they open with a specific technical claim, a policy development, or a number that signals insider knowledge.

**Example 1 — The technical explainer hook:**
"A grid-scale battery responds to a frequency event in under 100 milliseconds.
Here is what happens in that window — and why it matters for grid stability."

**Example 2 — The policy impact opener:**
"FERC Order 2023 just changed the interconnection queue for every utility in the country.
Most project developers haven't modeled the timeline implications yet.
Here is what the math looks like."

**Example 3 — The numbers-first sustainability hook:**
"We brought 120 MW of solar online at $38/MWh LCOE.
18 months from site control to commercial operation.
Here is what made that timeline possible."

**Example 4 — The personal profile technical authority hook:**
"I've designed transmission infrastructure for 14 years.
The most common reason projects stall has nothing to do with permitting.
Here is the factor most developers underestimate."

## Industry-specific CTA patterns

Energy and utilities CTAs work best when they invite peer technical commentary, drive saves on regulatory reference content, or signal that more detailed analysis is available.

**Technical peer prompt:**
"What interconnection queue timelines are you seeing in your region? Comparing notes across three ISOs."

**Policy reference save-bait:**
"Save this if you are tracking IRA provision implementation. I'll update the table as final rules come out."

**Personal disclaimer close:**
"Opinions are my own, not those of [employer]. For formal regulatory positions, see [employer]'s filed comments at FERC."

**Resource CTA:**
"Full LCOE comparison is in the first comment. Methodology is in the appendix. Primary data from NREL."

## Compliance rules

**FERC market manipulation rules.** The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission's anti-manipulation rules under the Federal Power Act restrict what market-facing employees and traders at utilities can post about pricing, market conditions, and anticipated supply or demand shifts. A LinkedIn post by a utility trader discussing anticipated congestion on a specific transmission path or anticipated pricing in a specific market hub can constitute market manipulation depending on the circumstances. Trading and market operations employees should confirm permitted content scope with their compliance function.

**NERC CIP — Critical Infrastructure Protection standards.** NERC CIP standards restrict imagery of bulk electric system assets including certain substations, control rooms, and cyber assets. Before posting any visual content from a regulated facility — including behind-the-scenes video, photograph tours, or infrastructure explainers — confirm with the facility's CIP compliance function that the specific imagery does not expose protected assets or information.

**SEC Regulation FD for publicly traded utilities.** Material non-public information must be disclosed through official channels — earnings releases, SEC filings, press releases — not through individual LinkedIn posts. An employee at a publicly traded utility who posts material information about the company's financial performance, generation outages affecting earnings, or contract awards before official disclosure creates Reg FD exposure for the company.

**State PUC advertising rules.** Many state public utility commissions restrict how regulated utilities can promote services, advertise rate structures, or market to customers. Content by employees of regulated utilities that could be construed as advertising about rate structures or service offerings may require review under the applicable state PUC rules.
