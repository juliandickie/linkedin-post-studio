# Legal — Content patterns and compliance

## What works

Legal on LinkedIn sits at an unusual intersection: the professional audience wants substantive analysis, but attorney advertising rules constrain how that analysis can be framed. Creators who build significant followings treat LinkedIn as a law review commentary venue, not a client-acquisition advertisement.

**Case and statute commentary.** The highest-performing legal content analyzes a recent court decision, regulatory shift, or statutory change with enough specificity to be useful to practitioners. A post that walks through a circuit split or explains why a Supreme Court decision creates downstream exposure in a specific practice area gets engagement from lawyers who rarely comment on anything. The post teaches; it does not advise.

**Deal trend and transaction commentary.** In-house and M&A practitioners engage with content documenting shifts in deal terms, market standards for reps and warranties, or post-closing dispute patterns. "I've seen the material adverse effect clause litigated four times this year. Here is what the cases reveal about how courts are reading the standard language" travels in transactional circles because practitioners recognize the pattern.

**Procedural insight posts.** Civil procedure, discovery mechanics, and motion practice are underserved on LinkedIn. Explaining what happens when a motion to compel goes unanswered is useful to junior practitioners, generates saves from associates, and positions the author as someone who has done the work.

**Practice management observation.** Law firm economics, partner compensation, lateral dynamics, and in-house operations generate intense engagement from lawyers who discuss these topics almost nowhere else. Sterling Miller's in-house content is the reference — operational specificity without confidential disclosure.

**Regulatory and policy commentary.** Antitrust enforcement shifts, FTC rulemaking, SEC examination priorities — these attract general counsel and compliance professionals who use LinkedIn as a news aggregation layer. The post must cite the specific development, not offer a general impression.

## What fails

**Client solicitation dressed as commentary.** "If you or anyone you know has been in an accident, here's what you need to know about your rights" is advertising under Rule 7.1, not education. Education addresses a legal principle; solicitation directs the reader toward a specific outcome that requires retaining counsel.

**Specialist claims without basis.** Referring to oneself as a "specialist" without board certification triggers Rule 7.2. Stick to experience framing: "I've handled 200 employment discrimination cases" rather than "I am an employment law specialist."

**LinkedIn DMs following up on posts.** Sending a DM to someone who commented and identified a legal problem can trigger Rule 7.3 in most states. This is one of the most commonly missed LinkedIn risks in legal practice.

**Testimonials without compliant disclaimers.** Dollar-amount testimonials are prohibited in North Carolina under 2012 FEO 1 and restricted elsewhere. All testimonials require "results may vary; past results do not guarantee future outcomes" language.

**Commentary on pending cases where you represent a party.** Rules of professional conduct restrict extrajudicial statements with a substantial likelihood of materially prejudicing a proceeding.

**Confidentiality breaches in case teardowns.** Anonymizing names is insufficient if other details — industry, deal size range, jurisdiction, outcome — allow identification.

## Industry-specific hook adaptations

Legal hooks perform best when they open with a named decision, a specific rule, or a precise observation from practice. The audience is skeptical of vague claims and responds to specificity that signals actual knowledge.

**Example 1 — The court decision opener:**
"A federal court just ruled on [specific case name] in a way most practitioners missed.
Here is the implication for every company using standard indemnification language."

**Example 2 — The practice observation hook:**
"I've reviewed 300 NDAs this year.
The same three clauses are creating problems in almost every negotiation.
Here is what they are and why they keep appearing."

**Example 3 — The deal term pattern:**
"The Material Adverse Effect clause was litigated more in 2023 than any prior year.
The courts are reading the standard language differently than most drafters assume.
Here is the pattern."

## Industry-specific CTA patterns

Legal CTAs must avoid direct solicitation. The most effective CTAs drive peer conversation, save-worthy reference behavior, or point to educational resources that do not require the reader to become a client.

**Peer commentary prompt:**
"What are you seeing in your practice on this? My read is based on three circuits. Curious whether it holds in yours."

**Save-bait for practitioner reference content:**
"Save this for the next time you are reviewing standard indemnification language. General commentary, not advice on your specific deal."

**Educational resource close:**
"The full opinion is in the first comment. Linked directly from the court's website."

**Soft close without solicitation:**
"This is general commentary on the developing law, not legal advice for your situation. If the question affects your organization, consult outside counsel with jurisdiction-specific experience."

## Compliance rules

**ABA Model Rule 7.1 — False or misleading communications.** A communication about a lawyer or law firm's services is prohibited if it contains a material misrepresentation or omits a fact necessary to make the statement, considered as a whole, not materially misleading. Applies to post body text, headline claims, and profile-level descriptions.

**ABA Model Rule 7.2 — Advertising.** Advertising is permitted subject to Rules 7.1 and 7.3. Prohibits compensation for recommendations outside enumerated exceptions. Claims of "specialist" status require board certification or state bar designation.

**ABA Model Rule 7.3 — Solicitation.** Attorneys may not solicit employment from a specific person via real-time electronic contact when a significant motive is pecuniary gain and the prospective client has not previously signaled interest. LinkedIn DMs responding to comments where the person has identified a legal problem are the primary risk vector.

**State filing requirements.** Florida requires many ads be submitted to The Florida Bar and restricts testimonials. Texas Rule 7.04 requires filing most paid communications. Louisiana Rule 7.2 requires the LSBA file number. Confirm filing obligations in each state of admission.

**New York Rule 7.4.** Requires prominent disclaimers for specialization claims — any communication implying specialization must state the attorney is not certified as a specialist by the state bar.

**Testimonial disclaimers.** All states permitting testimonials require prior-results disclaimers. North Carolina 2012 FEO 1 prohibits dollar-amount testimonials. Several other states restrict them similarly.

**California Rules 7.1 through 7.5.** California rejected the ABA's 2018 amendments and did not adopt the patent/admiralty specialist carve-outs. California-admitted attorneys must review the California Rules independently.

## Default disclaimers

- "This is general commentary, not legal advice. Consult counsel for your specific situation."
- "Attorney advertising. Prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome."
