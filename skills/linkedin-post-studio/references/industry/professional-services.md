# Professional Services — Content patterns and compliance

## What works

Professional services content on LinkedIn has a structural advantage: the audience already trusts practitioners over generalists. A consultant posting lessons from an actual client engagement, a CPA walking through a specific tax planning scenario, or an agency sharing campaign data from a recent project — all carry authority that no amount of brand-level content can replicate. The format that exploits this most effectively is insight with receipts.

- **Short case vignettes with anonymized details.** The anonymized case — "A mid-market manufacturer came to us with a 14-month DSO problem. Here is the three-step process that got it to 38 days in two quarters" — gives readers a model they can apply to their own situation. The lesson is the product. Keep the vignette to three or four sentences; save the full detail for the follow-up conversation.

- **Frameworks with your name on them.** The positioning canvas, the agency pitch scoring rubric, the due diligence checklist — named proprietary frameworks perform materially better than generic "here are the steps" posts because they signal intellectual investment and make the post easier to save and reference. April Dunford's positioning work is the reference case here.

- **Contrarian commentary on industry norms.** "Most consultants charge by the hour because they don't believe in their own methodology" is a more engaging opening than a bulleted list of engagement models. Professional services audiences are composed of other practitioners who have strong views on how their field operates. Posts that challenge those views generate substantive comment threads.

- **Public reasoning about pricing, scope creep, and client dynamics.** Blair Enns has built an audience by writing publicly about pricing for creative services, why agencies discount, and the psychology of value. These topics are relevant to both potential clients and peers — and they signal a confident, well-considered practice.

- **Process transparency.** "Here's how we actually conduct a financial statement audit" or "Here's the checklist we use for every brand strategy engagement" gives prospects a window into working with you before they commit. This is the professional services equivalent of a product demo.

- **Specific performance data from client work.** "Three of the eight strategies we presented were implemented. Here is what the two that actually worked had in common." Specificity of outcome, even when the story involves partial success, builds more credibility than case studies that claim universal wins.

## What fails

- **Overly credentialed, formal prose that reads as a white paper summary.** The LinkedIn feed is not a conference proceedings. If the post requires a JD or CPA designation to parse the first paragraph, it will not earn the "See more" click from anyone outside your immediate peer group.

- **Testimonial posts without a lesson.** "Working with [client] was a privilege" performs worse than almost any alternative format. Quote a client inside a story, not as the entire post.

- **Service menu posts framed as content.** "We offer audit, tax, and advisory services to mid-market companies" is not a post — it is an ad with no conversion path. Professional services content converts by demonstrating expertise, not by listing capabilities.

- **Positioning that is indistinguishable from competitors.** "We help businesses grow" does not create preference. "We specialize in M&A accounting for PE-backed portfolio companies between $20M and $200M in revenue" creates preference. Vague positioning is an anti-pattern that affects both content performance and pipeline quality.

- **Case study posts that begin with the client outcome.** Opening with "We helped Company X achieve Y" buries the hook. The case study needs to begin with the problem, the stakes, or an observation about why conventional approaches fail.

- **Engagement bait disguised as thought leadership.** "Agree or disagree: most consultants are overpaid" is engagement bait with a professional veneer. It generates comments, but not from the people who become clients.

## Industry-specific hook adaptations

Professional services hooks should establish practitioner authority immediately, often through a pattern observation from client work. The hook should signal that what follows is grounded in real practice, not in frameworks borrowed from a business book.

**Example 1 — The practitioner observation:**
"I've reviewed 60 agency pitches this year.
Most lose before they say a word about price.
Here is the structural error I keep seeing."

**Example 2 — The counterintuitive finding:**
"The consultants who raise prices fastest are not the best consultants.
They're the ones who learned to say no first.
Here is the sequence."

**Example 3 — The case vignette opener:**
"A client called us nine months after the engagement ended.
Revenue was up. Their team hated us.
Here's what we learned about what success actually looks like."

**Example 4 — The industry norm challenge:**
"Most accounting firms won't tell you this:
The partner billing you learned your craft from a partner who learned from someone in 1987.
The methodology is older than the problems it's solving."

## Industry-specific CTA patterns

Professional services CTAs work best when they lower the commitment threshold for prospects while demonstrating that you are selective. The "DM me for a chat" approach signals desperation. A more specific ask signals confidence.

**Qualified inquiry trigger:**
"If you're a PE-backed operator trying to compress your close timeline before a liquidity event, book a 20-minute call through the link in my bio. We take four engagements per quarter."

**Save prompt for framework posts:**
"Save this checklist. It's the same one we use at the start of every brand strategy engagement."

**Engagement question for peer content:**
"How are you handling the scope creep conversation with clients this year? Something has shifted — curious what you're seeing."

**Soft close for educational posts:**
"This is a simplified version of the methodology. The full framework is in my newsletter — link in bio for the archive."

## Example creators to study

- **Codie Sanchez** (Contrarian Thinking) — acquisition entrepreneurship and holding company frameworks; blends specific deal data with strong editorial voice; demonstrates how practitioners can build media businesses around their domain expertise.
- **April Dunford** (Obviously Awesome) — positioning for tech companies; her LinkedIn content is a masterclass in turning a consulting methodology into public-facing intellectual property that creates inbound at scale.
- **Jon Younger** (freelance talent researcher) — independent work and consulting business models; strong analytical voice with academic grounding and practitioner pragmatism.
- **Rory Sutherland** (Ogilvy) — behavioral economics applied to marketing and consulting; contrarian reasoning at high volume; shows how strong editorial voice compounds over time.
- **Blair Enns** (Win Without Pitching) — pricing and business development for creative agencies; one of the clearest examples of how a consultant can make their methodology the content.
- **Mark Ritson** (former professor, Marketing Week columnist) — direct, evidence-based marketing commentary that challenges practitioners; the reference case for credentialed-but-accessible professional voice on LinkedIn.
