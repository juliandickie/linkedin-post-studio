# B2B SaaS — Content patterns and compliance

## What works

Founder-led narratives dominate B2B SaaS on LinkedIn. The more specific the post, the more it outperforms. Generic category-level commentary from a company page competes for attention against executives sharing real build stories — and loses badly. Here is what consistently outperforms:

- **Specific metrics with context.** ARR milestones, CAC payback periods, NRR benchmarks, and churn numbers all perform well when framed around a lesson or a turning point. "We grew 300%" fails. "We grew from $400K to $1.2M ARR in 14 months by cutting our ICP from 9 verticals to 2" succeeds because readers can extract a mental model.

- **Unbundled case studies with named customers.** Where NDAs permit, naming the customer and walking through the specific problem, approach, and measurable outcome generates more saves and profile visits than any other format. The unbundled version — one lesson per post instead of a full story — outperforms the full case study write-up.

- **"What failed and why" transparency posts.** Founders publishing honest failure post-mortems consistently outperform the same founders posting wins. The credibility signal is high because failure posts are relatively rare and feel unscripted. Show the actual numbers. Skip the redemption arc if it hasn't happened yet.

- **Product teardown commentary on competitors.** Peep Laja and Katelyn Bourgoin are primary references here. Short posts that diagnose why a competitor's onboarding, pricing page, or positioning fails read as both entertaining and educational. They signal deep domain expertise without promoting your own product.

- **Go-to-market framework sharing.** Positioning methodologies, pipeline segmentation approaches, self-serve versus sales-assisted decision trees. Frameworks with actual names and reusable structure get saved at high rates. The post's job is to make the reader feel smarter about their own GTM problem, not to pitch yours.

- **Pricing experiments with real numbers.** "We tested three pricing models over six months. Here's what actually happened to conversion, churn, and expansion revenue." Real pricing data is genuinely rare on LinkedIn. That scarcity drives outsized engagement.

## What fails

- **Anonymous corporate voice from a company page.** B2B SaaS company pages almost universally underperform personal founder or employee profiles. The algorithm rewards personal accounts already, and audiences trust people, not logos. Company page text-only posts deliver as low as 0.28x baseline reach.

- **Gated generic ebooks as lead magnets.** A "12 Tips for Better Sales Emails" PDF from a company page in exchange for an email address signals that the brand is more interested in your contact details than in actually helping you. The CTA lands flat and the post dies.

- **"Excited to announce" press-release-style posts.** Product launches written for a press release rather than a LinkedIn audience skip the part where the reader has a reason to care. Lead with what it means for the customer, not the milestone.

- **Vague metric claims without denominator or context.** "We grew 300%" without a starting base, timeframe, or definition of the metric signals either insecurity about the numbers or a lack of respect for the reader's intelligence. Either interpretation suppresses engagement.

- **Feature announcement posts written like changelog entries.** "We just shipped X, Y, and Z" belongs in your product newsletter. On LinkedIn, the post needs a human story attached: what customer pain drove the decision, what tradeoffs were made, what it means for a specific persona.

- **Thought leadership that could be from anyone.** "Great teams have great culture" and similar content costs nothing to write and carries no signal about your actual expertise. If any of your 5,000 competitors could have published it, delete it.

## Industry-specific hook adaptations

B2B SaaS hooks work best when they front-load a specific number, a counterintuitive claim, or a decision that had real stakes. Readers in this space are trained to detect and skip low-signal content fast.

**Example 1 — The specific metric reversal:**
"We cut our ICP from 9 industries to 2.
Revenue went up 40% in 90 days.
Here's what we actually changed."

**Example 2 — The failure opener:**
"We spent $2.1M on paid acquisition last year.
It generated negative payback at 24 months.
Here is exactly where the model broke."

**Example 3 — The product teardown opener:**
"HubSpot's free tier is not generosity.
It's a 3-year land-and-expand play.
Here is how it works."

**Example 4 — The founder candor hook:**
"I fired my VP of Sales in month 8.
Best decision I made all year.
Worst month of my career getting there."

## Industry-specific CTA patterns

CTAs in B2B SaaS perform best when they offer something specific and diagnostic. Generic "follow me for more" requests are dead. The highest-performing patterns here involve a keyword trigger or a direct question that qualifies the reader.

**DM keyword trigger:**
"If you're struggling with CAC payback at Series A, DM me 'AUDIT' and I'll send you the 20-question diagnostic we use with clients."

**Framework save prompt:**
"Save this before your next pipeline review. The math changes fast when you add expansion revenue to the model."

**Engagement question close:**
"What's the metric your board watches most closely right now? Drop it in the comments — I'm curious whether churn or NRR is winning."

**Soft close for awareness posts:**
"If this framing helped, the full positioning methodology is in my newsletter. Link in bio."
