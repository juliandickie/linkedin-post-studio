# E-commerce / DTC — Content patterns and compliance

## What works

LinkedIn is not a consumer channel for e-commerce. Shoppers do not browse LinkedIn to find products. The audience here is operators: founders, CMOs, heads of growth, agency partners, investors, and vendors serving the DTC and e-commerce space. This distinction shapes every content decision. The content that performs is operator-to-operator. It assumes the reader knows what CAC means, has run paid media, and is dealing with real operational problems today.

- **CAC/LTV math with actual numbers.** "Our blended CAC is $62. LTV at 12 months is $210. Here is how we got the LTV curve to bend in month four." This is the content that operators save and return to. The math must be real. Aspirational numbers without denominators are identified immediately by a sophisticated audience.

- **Meta and paid creative teardowns.** What is working in paid creative right now, why it works, and what the failure mode looks like. Barry Hott and Sarah Levinger are reference creators here. Their posts break down ad creative with the same rigor a product designer brings to UX critique: specific observations about hooks, image composition, offer structure, and the emotional mechanism the ad is exploiting.

- **Shopify stack and tech stack discussions.** "We switched from Klaviyo to [X] for SMS and here is what changed in revenue per recipient." Platform decisions, integration choices, and tech stack tradeoffs are highly searchable content for operators evaluating the same decisions. These posts get saved at high rates.

- **Retention curves and subscription business mechanics.** Cohort analysis, survival curves, pause versus cancel behavior, and what changes the retention inflection point. Subscription-model DTC is a distinct operator segment with specific analytics and specific interventions. Content that addresses these mechanics precisely outperforms generic "improve your LTV" advice.

- **Post-mortem content on failed launches or product lines.** What the pre-launch thesis was, what the numbers looked like at week two, and what the data said about why it did not work. Failure post-mortems in e-commerce are rare and therefore high-signal. Operators share them because the lessons are expensive to learn independently.

- **Supply chain and ops decisions with financial stakes.** Inventory positioning decisions, 3PL transitions, shipping carrier negotiations, landed cost changes. The operational depth of e-commerce content is what separates it from the consumer-facing version of the same industry.

## What fails

- **Consumer-facing product content on a LinkedIn profile.** "Check out our new summer line" is the wrong content for the wrong audience. The LinkedIn feed is full of operators, investors, and vendors. They are not there to buy your product; they may be there to invest in, partner with, or hire from your company.

- **Agency-written brand voice posts from a founder account.** LinkedIn audiences in e-commerce are sophisticated about authentic operator voice. Polished brand prose published under a founder's name reads as ghostwritten and generates skepticism rather than engagement.

- **Vanity metrics without context.** "We did $5M in revenue last year" without AOV, margin, CAC, or channel breakdown is not useful to an operator audience. They want to know how, not just how much.

- **Lifestyle-first DTC content.** Aspirational product photography and brand storytelling work on Instagram. On LinkedIn, it looks like you are talking to the wrong audience. The content needs to be about the business, not the brand.

- **Generic advice about "knowing your customer."** The e-commerce operator audience has heard customer-centricity advice thousands of times. Content that repeats received wisdom without adding a specific mechanism, data point, or counterintuitive finding performs poorly.

- **Promotional posts for your own products.** LinkedIn does not convert well for direct e-commerce sales. Posts that read as ads suppress engagement because the audience expects editorial content, not product promotion. Even major DTC brands see poor direct ROI from product-promotion posts on LinkedIn.

## Industry-specific hook adaptations

E-commerce hooks work best when they front-load a specific operational number or a decision with real financial consequences. The reader is an operator who filters content fast. Generic hooks will not earn the "See more" click.

**Example 1 — The CAC/LTV math hook:**
"Our CAC went from $48 to $91 in six months.
We didn't cut the channel.
We changed one variable. Here's what it was."

**Example 2 — The creative teardown opener:**
"This ad has run for 11 months on Meta.
No fatigue. $2.1M in attributed revenue.
Here is why it works when everything else burned out."

**Example 3 — The ops decision with stakes:**
"We switched 3PLs in Q3.
We almost destroyed BFCM.
Here is the decision framework we wish we'd used."

**Example 4 — The retention insight hook:**
"Our month-2 churn was 38%.
We fixed it with one email.
Not the email you'd guess."

## Industry-specific CTA patterns

E-commerce CTAs on LinkedIn work best for building an operator-to-operator network, collecting DM conversations with peers and partners, and growing a newsletter or community of fellow operators.

**Save prompt for data-heavy posts:**
"Save this for your Q4 planning meeting. The math changes once you factor in contribution margin at scale."

**DM trigger for agency or vendor relationships:**
"If you're working through this same CAC problem and want a second set of eyes on the model, DM me 'CAC' and I'll send the spreadsheet we use."

**Engagement question close:**
"What's your current month-12 LTV benchmark? Drop it in the comments — building a data set across categories."

**Newsletter CTA for operator community:**
"I break down one e-commerce metric each week with real operator data. Subscribe via the link in bio."
