# Generic (fallback) — Content patterns for unspecified industries

This module applies when no industry-specific module exists for the user's niche. It covers broadly applicable LinkedIn content principles. For regulated industries (healthcare, legal, finance, real estate, manufacturing, energy-utilities, or similar), consider adding a custom industry module — see the note at the bottom of this file.

## What works

**Specificity over generality.** The single most reliable predictor of LinkedIn post performance across all industries is specificity. "I increased retention by 34% in 90 days using three changes" outperforms "here is how to improve retention" every time. Specific numbers, named methods, real timeframes, and credible outcomes signal that the post is grounded in actual experience, not general observation.

**Contrarian takes grounded in experience.** A declarative reversal of a common assumption performs well when the claim is defensible and the author can back it up. The key condition: the contrarian position must follow from something the author has actually observed or done, not from the desire to generate engagement. "Most onboarding programs fail for the same preventable reason" is a post. "Unpopular opinion: onboarding doesn't matter" is engagement bait.

**Frameworks you have actually used.** A three-step framework the author developed from real work outperforms a borrowed framework presented without attribution. The audience can tell the difference. "Here is the process I use to qualify a sales call in the first five minutes" is strong because it comes from a specific professional context and the detail reflects actual practice.

**Stories with real stakes.** A first-person account with a genuine consequence — revenue lost, client fired, hire that didn't work out, decision that cost time or money — generates more engagement than a success story without friction. The stakes make the lesson credible.

**Visible proof.** Screenshots of actual results (with sensitive data redacted), named clients with permission, specific outcome numbers, and dated timeframes all strengthen a post's credibility. Vague results ("significantly improved") generate less engagement than specific results ("from 12% to 31% in one quarter").

## What fails

**Generic motivational content.** "Your Monday mindset sets the tone for the week" and similar posts perform poorly across all professional niches. The LinkedIn algorithm deprioritizes low-comment content, and motivation-without-substance rarely generates substantive comments.

**"Unpopular opinion" openers.** The audience recognizes this as a signal for content that is not actually contrarian or not actually supported. If the take is genuine, state the position directly rather than announcing it is contrarian.

**Stacked arrow bullets without connective tissue.** Long lists of disconnected bullet points with arrow symbols before each one — a format known colloquially as "broetry" — have degraded significantly in engagement performance since 2023. Post-2024 audiences penalize this format when it hides a lack of substance beneath structural formatting.

**Manufactured vulnerability.** A post that performs vulnerability without real stakes — a decision that felt hard but had no actual cost, a lesson that carries no evidence of the failure that generated it — reads as performance. Genuine vulnerability has a consequence visible in the story.

**Rage-bait generalizations.** Posts making sweeping negative claims about a category of people — managers, founders, a profession — to generate angry comments are increasingly penalized by the LinkedIn algorithm. Comments from irritated readers do not carry the same weight as substantive replies.

## Industry-specific CTA patterns

Standard CTA patterns apply across all industries. The strongest performers:

- **DM keyword triggers:** "Comment [WORD] and I'll send [resource]." Drives comments and DM volume simultaneously.
- **Comment-bait:** A direct question at the end of the post that invites a specific, answerable response — not "what do you think?" but "what is your current process for X?"
- **Save-bait:** Explicitly inviting saves on reference content ("save this for your next [situation]").
- **Soft close:** Mentioning a service or product in context without a direct sales ask, typically in the last one or two lines.

For full CTA pattern documentation and examples, see `references/post-structure/post-structure-index.md`.

## Example creators to study

There is no universal reference creator for a generic industry module. Instead: search LinkedIn for five to ten practitioners in your specific role and topic area. Filter by engagement rate rather than follower count. Look for creators whose posts consistently generate substantive comments from people who appear to be practitioners in the same field — this is the signal that the content is working at the peer level, not just generating broad impressions.
