# Real Estate — Content patterns and compliance

## What works

Real estate on LinkedIn splits between operator-facing content (agents, brokers, investors, proptech builders) and consumer-adjacent content (buyers, sellers, renters). LinkedIn skews heavily toward the operator side. The content that performs is data-rich, specific to geography or deal mechanics, and treats practitioners as the primary audience rather than the public.

**Pop-culture crossovers.** Property valuation commentary tied to trending entertainment travels far outside the usual real estate audience. "What the Bridgerton estate would actually sell for in the current London market" or "The Succession penthouse — what that floor plan costs per square foot in 2024" pulls attention from audiences who would not normally engage with real estate content. These posts reach journalists and generate secondary distribution as news items.

**Hyper-local data stories.** ZIP code-level median price data combined with local labor market data creates content that journalists pick up. "Median home price in [zip code] vs. median tech salary in that submarket — the gap that explains the migration pattern" is specific enough to be useful and broad enough to travel. Vague regional commentary does not perform; specific market comparisons do.

**Virtual tours and neighborhood highlights.** Native vertical video under 60 seconds outperforms platform-linked content from Zillow or Realtor.com. A walkthrough that pairs one surprising data point with the visual — "this block's median sale price dropped 12% while this ZIP code gained 8%; here is what explains it" — performs substantially better than unanchored property footage.

**Transaction teardowns.** Anonymized breakdown of a closed deal — purchase price, carrying costs, renovation spend, exit value, timeline — with specific lessons extracted from the numbers. The deal must be genuine and the numbers credible. Fabricated or rounded examples are recognized immediately by the practitioner audience.

**Proptech and market structure commentary.** Analysis of how commission structures, NAR settlement implications, iBuyer dynamics, or new listing platform entrants change the agent business generates high engagement from the operator community. Mike DelPrete's proptech analysis is the reference. This content treats the industry as a business to be analyzed, not a lifestyle to be celebrated.

## What fails

**Generic "Just listed" posts.** A photo and an address without context, data, or a practitioner insight generates minimal engagement from the LinkedIn operator audience. This format belongs on Instagram or Nextdoor. On LinkedIn, the same property becomes interesting only when paired with specific market observations or deal mechanics.

**Motivational inspiration without grounding.** "Hustle until your dream home is yours" and similar real estate inspiration quotes have no practitioner value. The LinkedIn audience for real estate content is overwhelmingly composed of agents, investors, proptech operators, and industry professionals who respond to substance over motivation.

**Testimonial-only posts without a lesson.** A client testimonial with no data, no framework, and no practitioner insight performs below average. If the testimonial is the content, pair it with the deal mechanic, the market condition that made the transaction difficult, or the negotiation detail that resolved the obstacle.

## Industry-specific hook adaptations

Real estate hooks perform best when they open with a specific data point or a deal observation that signals practitioner knowledge.

**Example 1 — The hyper-local data opener:**
"Median home price in [zip code] just crossed $1.2M.
Median household income in that zip: $94K.
Here is the math on who is actually buying."

**Example 2 — The deal mechanic hook:**
"I just closed a deal that took 11 months to get to the table.
The obstacle had nothing to do with financing.
Here is what actually held it up."

**Example 3 — The pop-culture crossover hook:**
"The Succession penthouse floor plan is 6,800 sq ft in Midtown.
Current comparable asks in that building: $22M–$27M.
Here is the market context behind that number."

**Example 4 — The market structure take:**
"NAR's settlement just changed the math on buyer agent commissions.
Most agents I've spoken with haven't modeled out what it means for their revenue.
Here is the calculation."

## Industry-specific CTA patterns

Real estate CTAs work best when they invite peer data-sharing, drive saves on reference content, or direct operator-audience members to a resource.

**Peer data prompt:**
"What are you seeing in your market on days on market right now? Comparing notes across three metros."

**Save-bait for data reference posts:**
"Save this if you are tracking [metro] pricing. I update the chart monthly."

**DM keyword trigger:**
"If you want the full ZIP-level dataset, comment DATA and I'll send it over."

**Operator resource close:**
"Full commission structure breakdown is in the first comment. Not legal or financial advice. Talk to your broker and your tax adviser before making changes."

## Compliance rules

**Fair Housing Act.** All real estate marketing content must avoid language that implies preference for or against any protected class. This includes seemingly neutral descriptors that code for protected characteristics — phrases like "great for families," "quiet neighborhood," or "perfect for professionals" have generated Fair Housing complaints in other advertising contexts. When in doubt, describe the property, not the buyer.

**State broker licensing disclosure.** California Business and Professions Code 10140.6 requires disclosure of broker licensing information in all real estate advertising, including online content. Florida 475.25(1)(c) and most other states have analogous rules requiring that the licensed broker's name be disclosed in any advertisement. Include state and license number in the post or in profile disclosures that accompany every post.

**RESPA Section 8.** The Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act restricts kickbacks and unearned fees between settlement service providers. Any sponsored or paid partnership post involving a mortgage lender, title company, or other settlement service provider must disclose the paid relationship. Failure to disclose violates RESPA regardless of whether the content is technically advertising.
