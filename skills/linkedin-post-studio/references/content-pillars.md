# Content pillars (13 archetypes + 6-pillar system)

## The 13 durable pillar archetypes

**1. Personal story / origin**

First-person narrative about a turning point. Uses vulnerability as authority. Best used 2–4 times per month — saturation kills impact.

Examples: B2B SaaS founder — "In 2019 I was a broke engineer at Facebook. Today we just crossed $10M ARR. Here's what I wish someone told me." Healthcare practitioner — "I burned out in residency. Walked away from medicine for 2 years. Came back to build a concierge practice." Agency owner — "I fired 60% of my clients in January. Revenue grew 3x by September."

**2. Case study / client win**

Specific numbers, before and after, named client (with permission) or anonymized with enough detail to be credible. Highest-conversion pillar for service businesses.

Examples: B2B SaaS — "Fintech Series B reduced CAC by 47% in 90 days using [specific method]." Ecommerce — "DTC brand doing $40M/year cut ad spend by 22% and held revenue flat. Here's how." Dental — "Practice in Tampa added $380K in annual revenue with one procedural addition."

**3. Contrarian / hot take**

Declarative reversal of consensus. Highest ceiling for reach, highest risk of rage-bait. Must be defensible with data or experience.

Examples: B2B marketing — "Marketing attribution is a lie your CMO tells themselves." Finance — "Index fund maximalism is a millennial religion, not a strategy." Consulting — "Most corporate strategy decks are theater. Here's what actually moves revenue."

**4. Framework / how-to**

Named, numbered system the reader can copy. A named framework becomes associated with the creator's name in the market — it compounds authority over time.

Named examples: Justin Welsh's ContentOS and Content Matrix. Sahil Bloom's Luck Razor and 1-1-1 Method. Lara Acosta's SLAY framework. Nicolas Cole's Hook-Story-Offer. Dickie Bush's Atomic Essay.

Industry adaptations: Sales — "The 3-question discovery framework I use on every first call." Healthcare — "The 5-minute patient history framework that catches 80% of missed diagnoses." Real estate — "The 4-metric pre-offer diligence checklist."

**5. List post**

Numbered insights, scannable on mobile. Durable format because it matches how people consume on a phone.

Examples: B2B SaaS — "7 pricing mistakes every seed-stage founder makes." Legal — "11 contract clauses every founder should memorize." Ecommerce — "9 Shopify apps we deleted last quarter and why."

**6. Behind-the-scenes / building in public**

Transparency content. Attracts peers and industry watchers. Strong for founder-led brands.

Examples: Creator economy — "Here's the exact P&L of my 12-person creator team last month." Startup — "We just shipped v2 of our onboarding. Here's what changed and why." Agency — "Our quarterly team offsite retro, every metric, every miss, every win."

**7. Industry news commentary**

Low effort, moderate engagement, low conversion. Filler pillar — best for staying visible during idea droughts.

Examples: AI / tech — "Anthropic just announced X. Here's what most people are missing about the implications." Healthcare — "Medicare announced the 2026 reimbursement schedule. Three changes every practice should know." Legal — "Supreme Court ruling on X just changed how we should all think about Y."

**8. Data / research post**

Proprietary numbers are uncopyable and therefore the biggest competitive moat. Original data can define a niche.

Examples: B2B SaaS — "We analyzed 500 SaaS pricing pages. Here are the 7 patterns winners share." Creator — "I tested 50 hook formats over 90 days. Here's what actually worked." Healthcare — "We reviewed outcomes from 1,200 patients on [treatment protocol]. Here's what we learned."

**9. Failure / lesson learned**

Substitutes for personal story when vulnerability is needed without full origin. Use sparingly and only when the lesson is specific.

Examples: Startup — "We raised $4M at a $20M valuation. Then burned it in 14 months. Here's what I got wrong." Agency — "I hired a VP of Ops who cost us $800K and 6 months. Here's what I missed in the interview." Creator — "My first cohort flopped. 73 signups, 11 completions. Here's what I redesigned."

**10. Prediction / future of X**

Executives use this heavily because it signals vision and forward-thinking. High authority signal when it is informed.

Examples: B2B SaaS — "In 18 months, PLG will be dead as a default strategy. Here's why." Healthcare — "By 2028, 40% of primary care visits will be async. Three things that will change." Marketing — "The 'content calendar' as we know it is dead in 24 months."

**11. Swipe file / curation**

Borrowed credibility for creators who lack original experience in a domain. Best for newer creators building credibility through curation.

Examples: Design — "10 landing pages I've saved this month for reference." Sales — "7 cold email openers I've screenshotted this year." Writing — "The 5 newsletters I read every week without fail."

**12. Teach-what-you-know vs. share-what-you're-doing**

Justin Welsh's split. Target approximately 60% teach, 40% share. Teach-what-you-know builds authority. Share-what-you're-doing builds relatability. Neither alone is sufficient — authority without personality loses retention; relatability without expertise loses trust.

**13. Employee advocacy**

Fastest-growing pillar in 2025 because company pages are deprioritized by the algorithm. Employees posting from personal accounts amplify company reach by 30% on average even when only 3% participate. The leverage is asymmetric: a small number of employees who post consistently creates far more reach than a company page posting daily.

Examples: B2B SaaS — engineers posting about product launches from personal accounts dramatically outperform the company page post. Healthcare — clinicians posting patient-friendly explainers of company research. Legal — associates posting commentary on notable firm case wins.

## The six-pillar system (Problems, Processes, Proof, Perspective, Personal, Participation)

A compressed alternative to the 13 archetypes. Maps pillars directly to business outcomes. Useful at the planning level where granular archetype distinctions would slow down calendar construction.

**Problems** — Primary outcome: lead generation. Formats: text or carousel. Angle: "why this common process fails."

**Processes** — Primary outcome: saves and shares. Formats: carousel, document, text. Angle: "a 5-step way to decide X."

**Proof** — Primary outcome: trust and conversion. Formats: text, multi-image, video. Angle: "what changed after we did Y."

**Perspective** — Primary outcome: thought leadership. Formats: text, video. Angle: "why the market is misreading Z."

**Personal** — Primary outcome: brand affinity. Formats: text, video. Angle: "a lesson learned the hard way."

**Participation** — Primary outcome: reach and research. Formats: poll, question post, comments-led post. Angle: "how are you handling X in 2026?"

**When to use which model:** Use the six-pillar system at the planning layer — initial content strategy, pillar doc, coaching new creators who need simple category boundaries. Use the 13 archetypes at the drafting layer where granular pattern-matching matters. Many practitioners use the six pillars as the planning framework and the 13 archetypes as the execution framework. The two models are not in conflict; they operate at different levels of abstraction and should be used accordingly.

**Industry examples of six-pillar mix:**

- B2B SaaS founder (lead-gen-heavy): 30% Problems, 20% Processes, 20% Proof, 10% Perspective, 10% Personal, 10% Participation.
- Consultant (thought leadership): 15% Problems, 15% Processes, 15% Proof, 30% Perspective, 15% Personal, 10% Participation.
- Agency brand account (awareness): 10% Problems, 15% Processes, 15% Proof, 15% Perspective, 25% Personal, 20% Participation.

## Balancing pillars for different goals

**Lead generation (founder selling a service):** 50% framework/how-to, 20% case study, 15% contrarian, 15% personal story. The "authority plus proof" mix. Frameworks show you can teach, case studies show results, contrarian takes differentiate, personal stories build human connection.

**Thought leadership (executive):** 40% contrarian or prediction, 30% data/research, 20% personal story, 10% list/teardown. Executives need to demonstrate vision, original insight, and pattern recognition across their industry.

**Brand awareness (top-of-funnel):** 40% story, 30% list/curation, 20% hot take on news, 10% framework. Prioritizes memorability and shareability over conversion — stories travel further than frameworks.

## Ratio frameworks

**4-1-1 rule:** For every 6 posts, 4 are curated or educational, 1 is a soft sell, 1 is a hard sell. Prevents the feed from becoming a sales channel. Audiences who see too many commercial posts disconnect from both the person and the product; the 4-1-1 ratio keeps the relationship funded.

**3-2-1 weekly:** 3 educational, 2 personal insight, 1 personal story. Popular for executive accounts because it balances authority with humanity without over-indexing on either.

**Justin Welsh's 4-outcome split:** Every post must achieve at least one of four outcomes — entertain, educate, inspire, or convince. If the post achieves none of the four, cut it from the calendar. This rule eliminates filler content before it gets published. Apply it at the idea stage, not after the post is drafted, to avoid wasted drafting cycles.

## Vulnerability guardrail

If removing the first-person emotional opener doesn't change the post's lesson, the vulnerability is gratuitous. The red line: using a loved one's serious illness, death, or mental health crisis as a hook with no reader benefit. Reddit and ghostwriter communities call this "grief baiting."

Practical test: ask a trusted peer to read the post and answer, "Would you say this in a boardroom with industry peers watching?" If the answer is no, rewrite or cut.

## Why generic motivational content is dead in 2025–2026

Several trends converged to collapse the value of generic inspiration:

- LinkedIn's 2025 algorithm update deprioritizes broad engagement in favor of niche-relevant content. Broad motivational posts surface to too many unqualified audiences to earn quality-signal engagement.
- AI-generated content flooded the feed with generic inspiration throughout 2024, collapsing signal-to-noise on motivation content specifically.
- Audiences now read "resilience and adaptability" language as an AI tell, which actively reduces credibility.
- Lara Acosta explicitly warns against "cookie-cutter fluffy advice" in her public teaching on LinkedIn content strategy.

What works instead: tie every piece of advice to a specific example, number, or named person. "Be resilient" is dead. "In January 2023 I had $1,200 in my account and a mortgage due. Here's exactly what I did" works because it is verifiable, specific, and comes from a position the reader can picture.

The rule: if the post could have been written by anyone with a basic knowledge of your industry, it should not be published.

## Contrarian vs. rage-bait distinction

**Strong contrarian:**

- Specific, falsifiable claim against consensus.
- Backed by experience or data.
- Offers a constructive reframe.
- Challenges ideas, not identities.

Example (good): "Most SaaS companies are wrong about churn. Churn isn't a retention problem, it's a sales-qualification problem. Here's the data from 40 engagements."

**Rage-bait:**

- Sweeping generalization about a group — job title, generation, industry, gender.
- Designed to provoke defensive piling-on.
- No constructive reframe or alternative offered.

Example (bad): "Millennials are ruining the workplace. Here are 5 reasons."

Ghostwriter test: would the client say this in a board meeting with peers watching? If not, rewrite the angle.
